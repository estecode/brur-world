# BRUR Gameplay Research Map

Status: v0.1 — living research document

## Purpose

This document maps recurring patterns reported in Swedish news about criminal networks during roughly 2011–2026 into possible gameplay capabilities and consequence systems for BRUR.

It is research input, not a plan to recreate specific real crimes, victims, perpetrators, or organizations. Real cases are used to identify general patterns that can inform fictional systems and scenarios.

## Core design principle

> **BRUR depicts criminal environments without romanticizing them. Violence and crime may be central gameplay mechanics, but they should have credible human, social, and material consequences.**

Player agency should remain broad. The game should not become a moral lecture or make criminal gameplay artificially unplayable. Consequences should primarily emerge from the world, its systems, relationships, law enforcement, and the longer-term effects of player actions.

## Time development to preserve

BRUR should not model gang-related crime as one static phenomenon.

### Earlier 2010s

Research should cover local conflicts, drug markets, shootings, personal disputes, debts, weapons, witnesses and retaliation. Historical reporting needs further source coverage before this section is considered complete.

### 2018–2021: shootings and explosions as major recurring phenomena

SVT's reporting based on police statistics shows high levels of shootings and explosions. SVT reported 349 confirmed shootings between 1 January and 15 December 2020, with 44 deaths in that period, and cited research identifying at least 46 outsiders affected by gang-related attacks since 2011. SVT also reported 139 explosions in 2019, 98 in 2020 and 82 through 30 November 2021.

Gameplay implication: violence must interact with civilians, property, witnesses, emergency response, investigations and persistent consequences rather than existing only as combat between factions.

### 2020s: digital recruitment and distributed execution

Sveriges Radio has reported that recruitment of children and young people increasingly takes place through social media and then moves into encrypted apps. Reporting describes offers of money, threats when a recruited child hesitates, and a large pool of young people available for criminal assignments.

Gameplay implication: a criminal organization need not recruit only through physical street encounters. Communication, coercion, distance and replaceable young executors can be systemic parts of the fictional world.

## Emerging gameplay loop

Avoid a simple loop such as:

```text
commit crime -> earn money -> wanted level -> escape -> reset -> repeat
```

A more appropriate systemic direction is:

```text
need / belonging / money
        -> contacts
        -> small criminal tasks
        -> debt / loyalty / status
        -> greater responsibility
        -> money / influence
        -> conflict
        -> violence
        -> police / retaliation
        -> consequences for relationships and surroundings
        -> arrests / losses / power vacuums / new conflicts
```

A high criminal reputation should therefore not simply mean "winning". It may create access, money and influence while simultaneously increasing obligations, enemies, police attention, fear in ordinary relationships, and risks to people around the player.

The anti-glorification principle should be expressed through these systems and consequences rather than through moralizing messages.

## Research dimensions to expand

Future versions should deepen the map along the full lifecycle rather than focusing only on spectacular violent events:

```text
recruitment
-> first tasks
-> money / status
-> drug economy
-> debts
-> organization / hierarchy
-> territory / markets
-> conflict
-> violence
-> retaliation
-> police investigation
-> arrest / prison
-> relatives / civilians
-> exit programs / leaving crime
-> power vacuum / succession
```

Parallel research tracks should cover police work, victims and families, ordinary life around affected areas, criminal finances, prison, people leaving criminal networks, witnesses, social services and prevention.

## Research rules

1. Prefer Swedish primary news reporting, public-service reporting, official statistics and court/police material where appropriate.
2. Separate reported fact from gameplay interpretation.
3. Record dates so historical changes are not flattened into one timeless model.
4. Look for mundane structures and consequences, not only spectacular crimes.
5. Do not turn identifiable real victims or perpetrators into entertainment content.
6. Use real events to derive fictional systemic patterns rather than recreating individual crimes.
7. Continue to look for evidence that challenges the current model, including declining trends, successful prevention and people leaving criminal environments.
